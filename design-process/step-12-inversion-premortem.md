# THE TWO EXERCISES

**Inversion.** Instead of asking *"how do I make this succeed?"* — which produces vague optimism — ask *"how would I guarantee this fails?"* That question is much easier to answer well, because failure has specific, nameable causes. Then you check whether you are accidentally doing any of them.

**Pre-mortem.** Imagine it is 17 August, you scored 40/105, and write the story of *why*. Not "we might run out of time" but an actual narrative with dates and specifics. Research on this technique is consistent: **imagining the failure as already having happened produces far more candid and specific reasons than asking people to predict risks.** The past tense unlocks honesty the future tense doesn't.

# PART 3 — THREE PRE-MORTEMS

## Pre-mortem 1: DIVERGENCE-Tax scores 40/105

*It is 17 August. Here is what happened.*

The pipeline worked. It genuinely did — seven nodes, clean structured output, the citation matcher caught three fabricated sections in testing. The team was proud of it on the 15th.

But the test corpus took six days instead of three, because writing ground truth for a blurry photograph turns out to be slow and boring and nobody wants to do it. So the evaluation ran on nine cases instead of thirty. The results table looked thin, and a judge noticed: *"nine cases isn't a measurement, it's a sample."* Technical Execution came in at 17/25.

The interface was a white page with Times New Roman and a table. Nobody had time. UX scored 6/15 from every judge — **24 points lost to a weekend of CSS that never happened.**

The video ran 4 minutes 20. The first ninety seconds explained Indian crypto taxation. Two of the four judges are not Indian and have never filed ITR-3. By the time the ₹41,150 appeared they had disengaged, and Impact came in at 13/25 with the note *"real but narrow; team could not size the affected population."*

In the Q&A slot, one person answered everything. When a judge asked the quietest member how the citation matcher worked, there was a pause. Presentation: 6/10.

And Bounty 1 closed on 9 August while everyone was writing prompts. **Twenty points gone, and the tiebreak with it.**

**Nothing in this story is about the idea being wrong.**

## Pre-mortem 2: Second Opinion scores 40/105

*It demoed beautifully. That was the problem.*

Ninety seconds: paste a ChatGPT answer, watch the tool dismantle it. The room liked it.

Then a judge asked: *"So this is a prompt that criticises another prompt?"* And there was no good answer, because that is what it is. Without documents there is nothing to detect as missing, no two valuations to compute, no uncertainty budget, no election to record. Every node that made the architecture interesting had nothing to operate on.

Technical Execution: 14/25 — *"the workflow is two steps and the second step is a critique, which is well-established."* Innovation: 8/15 — *"self-refinement is prior art the team themselves cited."*

**The tool was too easy to build, and that showed in the only category that measures building.**

## Pre-mortem 3: The Checklist scores 40/105

*It was the most useful thing anyone made, and it was in the wrong competition.*

Three CAs said they would actually use it. One asked for a copy. It solved a real problem with no AI at all.

And it was submitted to the **ML Prompt Engineering** track, where Technical Execution is judged on *"quality and structure of the prompt/workflow design; measurable improvement over a naive baseline."*

There was no prompt. There was no workflow. There was no baseline.

**Technical Execution: 4/25, from each of four judges. 84 points forfeited on day one, by choosing the wrong container for a good idea.**

# PART 4 — THE FIVE DEEPEST OBJECTIONS TO OUR CURRENT IDEA

Not execution risks. Structural ones, from first principles.

### ⚠️ Objection 1 — the one the interviews must test: **custom may have already filled the gap**

Our entire thesis rests on there being no prescribed method. **But a statutory gap and a practical gap are different things.**

If every CA in India says *"we use the exchange statement value, the department accepts it, it's settled in practice"* — then **custom has filled the lacuna** even though statute hasn't. And a de facto standard makes a de jure gap practically irrelevant.

This is the objection that could end the project, and **it is answerable only by asking practitioners.** It is now the single most important thing the interviews must find out.

**If practice has converged:** the project pivots to *"here is the convention everyone uses, here is that it has no statutory basis, and here is what happens when an officer challenges it."* Still a project. Different project.

### ⚠️ Objection 2 — the insurance adoption problem

Our value is realised only if the taxpayer is challenged — a low-probability event, years away. **People systematically under-buy protection against low-probability, high-cost, delayed harms.** It is one of the most robust findings in behavioural economics, and it is why flood insurance is under-purchased in flood zones.

So the *perceived* value at the moment of purchase is near zero even though the *expected* value is positive.

**This is the real reason the buyer must be the CA** — not because she has budget, but because she sees the aggregate across 300 clients, so for her it is not low-probability at all. We reached the right conclusion in Step 5 for a weaker reason. Now we have the actual mechanism.

### ⚠️ Objection 3 — our best example is evidence the market is dying

The 8.5% divergence happened **because** the ED raided crypto payment firms and supply tightened. **Our most compelling illustration is a symptom of the flow being actively suppressed.**

The more vivid our example, the more it demonstrates that the thing we serve is under attack.

**Answer:** the divergence is an *illustration* of a general property — no prescribed method — not the problem itself. The gap exists at the normal 3–4% premium too. Use the spike to make it visible; never make it the argument.

### ⚠️ Objection 4 — the product's best advice makes the product unnecessary

If the correct professional advice is *"don't accept crypto,"* then our most honest output is *"you shouldn't have done this."*

**Answer:** harm reduction. We serve people who already received it. But note this constrains the whole framing — **we can never be a tool that makes taking crypto look sensible.** Any marketing that implies "crypto payments made easy" is both dishonest and self-defeating.

### ⚠️ Objection 5 — nobody has to decide

Our output requires the user to *elect* a valuation method. **But the freelancer's actual behaviour is to not think about it at all.** Forcing a decision is friction, and friction is the most common cause of tools going unused.

**Answer, and it changes the design:** the election must be **one tap with a default**, not a form. Show the recommended method pre-selected, show the alternative next to it, and record that both were displayed. **The record we need is "you were shown both," not "you agonised."**

That is a genuinely better design than what we had, and it came from an objection.
