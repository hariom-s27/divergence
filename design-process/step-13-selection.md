# FIRST — A WARNING ABOUT THIS METHOD

**A weighted scoring matrix can be made to say anything.** You pick the criteria, you pick the weights, you pick the scores. If you already have a favourite, you will unconsciously nudge it to the top and then feel you have proved something.

I built this matrix, and I already had a recommendation. **So the matrix alone is not evidence.**

It is still worth doing, for one specific reason: **it forces you to notice where an option is weak on a dimension you were not thinking about.** That is what it is for. Not choosing — noticing.

So: matrix first, then **three independent cross-checks** that do not use scores at all. If all four agree, the decision is safe. If they disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

# PART 6 — BUT THE RECOMMENDATION WAS INCOMPLETE

"Take A's substance with B's framing" is right and it does not say **when to be which** — and that ambiguity would get you the worst of both.

If you are general throughout, you lose "identifiable people," which the rubric names explicitly. If you are specific throughout, you lose the panel. **The answer is sequencing, not blending.**

## Open specific · widen once · land specific

**Open specific.** A person with a name and a number. *Priya, Pune, $5,000, ₹41,150 apart.* Nobody disengages from a person.

**Widen once, and only once.** *"The reason this happens has nothing to do with crypto. Rules are written before the situations arrive, so every regulated field has gaps — and AI fills them by guessing. It happens in medicine, in insurance, in employment law."* Thirty seconds. One widening. **Do not keep widening — that is where overclaim lives.**

**Land specific.** *"We measured it. Thirty cases, published baseline, and here is where the simple version beat us."*

**This structure is why A+B works.** The specific ends give you *identifiable people* and *measurable evidence*. The wide middle gives you *scale* and *comprehension*. Neither framing alone can do both.

# PART 7 — THE FOUR DECISIONS

## 🔒 DECISION 1 — Framing: **A+B, sequenced**

Specific → general → specific. One widening only. Never open with the word "crypto."

---

## 🔒 DECISION 2 — Node count: **five model calls and two deterministic checks**

Not "seven nodes." That description was wrong and it was costing us.

**A model call and a piece of deterministic code are not the same kind of thing**, and lumping them together as "nodes" hides the most reassuring fact about the architecture: some parts cannot hallucinate because they are not models.

| | Component | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Intake + extraction *(merged)* | 🤖 Model |
| 2 | Gap detector | 🤖 Model |
| 3 | Dual valuation | 🤖 Model + arithmetic |
| 4 | Regime resolver — three regimes, scoped corpus per regime | 🤖 Model |
| 5 | Adversarial checker | 🤖 Model |
| **✓** | **Citation matcher** | ⚙️ **Deterministic — string match against corpus** |
| **✓** | **Gap constraint enforcer** | ⚙️ **Deterministic — rejects any conclusion depending on a missing field** |

**Merging intake and extraction saves a call.** Collapsing 5a/b/c into one resolver run three times with a scoped corpus saves two calls, and *keeps the contamination protection* because the corpus is still scoped per pass.

**Why this framing is better in Q&A:** *"Why seven nodes?"* becomes *"Five model calls, each doing one job, wrapped in two checks that are ordinary code and therefore cannot make things up."* An engineer hears that and relaxes.

---

## 🔒 DECISION 3 — Jurisdictions: **India only in the system. Singapore and UAE in one slide.**

Cut from the build entirely. Step 9 said "shallow tier B"; that was still too much.

**Keep them as researched context in the pitch:** *"The same payment resolves to two taxable events in India, one in Singapore, and none for a small freelancer in the UAE."* Stated as research, clearly not as system output.

**Zero build cost. Zero risk of the system producing a wrong foreign answer. Keeps the 2/1/0 visual**, which is genuinely striking.

**Your scalability proof is the non-crypto cross-border receipt**, which runs through the same pipeline unchanged. One proof, done properly, beats two done thinly.

---

## 🔒 DECISION 4 — Name: **keep DIVERGENCE, add a descriptor**

I considered changing it. The name describes the *symptom* — two numbers differ — rather than the *product*, which is a record of what you knew.

**But changing it now costs consistency across a dozen documents and buys very little.** And it pairs well with the dimension line in the interface.

**Fix it with a line rather than a rename:**

> ### DIVERGENCE
> *A record of what the law didn't decide.*

Memorable name, plain-English clarity, zero switching cost.

# PART 8 — THE PARAGRAPH

*This is the deliverable, and it doubles as your Q&A answer to "why this approach?"*

> We scored six options against the actual rubric and this one won on every check, but the reason is simpler than the matrix. Every other option gave something up. Going fully specific meant losing judges who have never filed an Indian tax return, and losing the scalability marks — a tool for a market that enforcement is shrinking scores badly on "path to scale." Going fully general meant giving up the only real evidence we have: a verified gap in a named rule, a dated 8.5% divergence, and case law in a tribunal's own words. Building something simpler meant giving up the category that measures building. Changing domain meant throwing away twelve steps of research two weeks out.
>
> So we kept the specific case and changed only when we say it. We open with a person — Priya in Pune, one invoice, ₹41,150 apart. We widen once to say why it happens: rules are written before the situations arrive, so every regulated field has gaps, and AI fills them by guessing. Then we come back and show the measurement. The specific ends give us people you can point at and numbers you can check. The wide middle gives us the scale. **Neither framing could have done both, and it cost us nothing to build, because it was never a product decision. It was a decision about the order we say things in.**
